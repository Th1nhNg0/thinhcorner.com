In [Silicon Valley](/writing/silicon-valley) I wrote about how the valley grew out of Stanford, defense money and venture capital. In [Tài sản trí tuệ](/writing/tai-san-tri-tue) I wrote about the invisible assets those companies are built on. This post is about the money in between: how a company with two people and no revenue ends up with millions of dollars from strangers, and what it gives up in return.

You see the headlines all the time. "X raises \$20M Series A." "Y becomes a unicorn." But the words around them (seed, pre-money, dilution, liquidation preference) are rarely explained. I wanted to understand them properly, so I read up and wrote it down.

## Part 1: Why startups don't just borrow

A normal small business, like a coffee shop, usually starts with savings and a bank loan. The bank looks at the cash flow, the collateral and the owner's history, and charges interest. If the shop does well, the bank gets its interest back. If it does very well, the bank still only gets its interest back.

A startup is a different kind of bet. Most of them fail, and the ones that work can grow a hundred times or more. A bank has no reason to take that risk: it can't get more than its interest if things go great, and it loses everything if they go badly. Most startups also have nothing to put up as collateral. The assets are code, a team and an idea.

So startups sell a piece of the company instead. This is **equity financing**. The investor gives cash now and gets shares. If the company fails, the shares are worth nothing and the founders owe nothing. If the company becomes huge, the investor's small piece becomes huge too. That payoff shape fits the risk, and it's why startup money and bank money behave so differently.

The people who do this for a living are **venture capitalists** (VCs). They manage funds that take money from big investors and put it into young, risky companies.

## Part 2: A short history

### The first VC fund

The modern VC business started in Boston, not California. In 1946 Georges Doriot, a Harvard Business School professor, founded **American Research and Development Corporation** (ARD) with Ralph Flanders, Karl Compton and others[^ard]. The idea was to fund new technology companies coming out of places like MIT, which banks wouldn't touch.

ARD's famous bet came in 1957. It put \$70,000 into Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC), a minicomputer company started by two MIT engineers, for a large share of the company[^ard]. When ARD sold its stake in 1972, it was worth more than \$400 million[^mittr]. That's over 5,000 times the money. One deal like that paid for every other bet ARD made, and it set the pattern the whole industry still follows.

### Fairchild and Sand Hill Road

Also in 1957, eight engineers left William Shockley's lab and wanted to start their own semiconductor company. A young banker named Arthur Rock, then at Hayden Stone in New York, helped them find a backer in Sherman Fairchild's camera company. Hayden Stone got 20% of the new company, **Fairchild Semiconductor**[^history]. Fairchild later spun out dozens of companies, including Intel, which Rock also helped fund.

In 1972 the first firms set up on Sand Hill Road in Menlo Park, starting with Kleiner Perkins and Sequoia Capital[^history]. The street is still where many of the biggest VC firms have their offices.

### Pension money arrives

For a long time VC was small. In 1975 the whole private equity industry raised only about \$10 million, because a new US law (ERISA) made pension funds afraid of risky investments. In 1978 the Labor Department relaxed the "prudent man" rule, which let pension funds put some money into venture funds. Fundraising went from \$39 million in 1977 to \$570 million in 1978[^history]. Pension funds, university endowments and insurance companies are still the main sources of VC money today.

### Y Combinator and the SAFE

The last big change was at the very early end. Y Combinator, started in 2005, gave small amounts of money to lots of very young startups in batches. In 2013 Carolynn Levy at YC created the **SAFE** (Simple Agreement for Future Equity), a short standard contract that lets a startup take money without setting a price for its shares yet[^safe]. It made the first round of money much faster and cheaper to close, and today it's the default for early rounds in the US.

## Part 3: The rounds

A startup usually raises money in steps, called rounds. Each round is meant to get the company to a point where it's worth a lot more, so the next round happens at a higher price. The names are loose, but they go roughly like this.

**Bootstrapping.** The founders use their own savings, or revenue from the first customers. No outside money, so no one to answer to. Plenty of good companies never leave this stage.

**Friends and family.** Small checks from people who trust the founder more than the business plan.

**Angels.** Wealthy individuals, often ex-founders, who invest their own money. A typical angel check is in the tens of thousands of dollars.

**Pre-seed and seed.** The first "real" money, often from small VC funds and angels together. The company may only have a prototype and a few users. In the US the seed round today is usually done with SAFEs or as a small priced round.

**Series A.** The company has found something that works (people use it, maybe pay for it) and needs money to turn that into a repeatable business. This is usually the first round led by a big VC fund, and the first time the investor gets a board seat.

**Series B, C, D and beyond.** Money to grow: hire, expand to new markets, buy competitors. Each letter is just the next round. Later rounds bring in bigger players like growth funds, banks and sovereign wealth funds.

**The exit.** Eventually investors want their money back. That happens when the company is sold (an acquisition, or M&A) or lists its shares on a stock exchange (an IPO). Until then, the shares are mostly paper.

## Part 4: Valuation and dilution

### Pre-money and post-money

Two numbers show up in every round.

- **Pre-money valuation** is what the company is worth before the new cash comes in.
- **Post-money valuation** is pre-money plus the new cash.

Say a startup agrees to a pre-money valuation of \$8 million and raises \$2 million. The post-money is \$10 million. The investor put in \$2 million of the \$10 million, so they now own 20% of the company. The founders still have the same number of shares, but those shares are now 80% of a bigger company instead of 100% of a smaller one.

That shrinking percentage is called **dilution**. It isn't automatically bad. Owning 80% of a \$10 million company is better than owning 100% of a company that runs out of money. The question is always whether the cash makes the company grow faster than your slice shrinks.

### How much founders give up

On Carta, the median startup sold 18.8% of the company in its seed round and 17.9% in its Series A in early 2025[^carta]. Stack two rounds like that and the founders are down to two thirds:

In real life it's worse, because of two more things.

**The option pool.** Startups pay people partly in stock options, so they set aside shares for future employees, often 10% to 20% of the company. Investors usually ask for the pool to be created *before* their money comes in, so it comes out of the founders' share, not theirs.

**SAFEs and convertible notes.** Money raised on a SAFE doesn't become shares until the next priced round. When it does convert, it does so at a discount or at a valuation cap, which means the early investors get extra shares and everyone else is diluted a bit more[^safe].

## Part 5: The fine print

The valuation is the number in the press release, but the term sheet has other terms that can matter more. A term sheet is the short document that sets out the deal before the lawyers write the full contracts.

### Preferred shares

VCs almost never buy the same shares the founders have. Founders and employees hold **common stock**. Investors buy **preferred stock**, which comes with extra rights. The most important one is the liquidation preference.

### Liquidation preference

A **liquidation preference** says who gets paid first when the company is sold or shut down. With a standard "1x" preference, the investor gets their money back before common shareholders get anything.

Say a VC put \$10 million into a company and the company sells for \$8 million. With a 1x preference, the VC takes all \$8 million. The founders and employees get zero, even if they owned 80% of the shares on paper.

Some deals have a 2x or 3x preference, where the investor gets two or three times their money first. Some are **participating**, which means the investor takes their money back first *and then* also shares in what's left as if they held common stock. These terms show up more when the market is bad and the company has little choice.

The best known example is Good Technology. It made secure email software, was valued at \$1 billion, and filed to go public in 2014. The IPO never happened. In 2015 it sold to BlackBerry for \$425 million[^goodvb]. Here is where that money went:

### Other terms

- Anti-dilution protects investors if a later round happens at a lower price (a "down round"). The earlier investors get extra shares to make up for it. The founders absorb the difference. Just over 19% of rounds on Carta in early 2025 were down rounds[^carta].
- A Series A lead usually takes a board seat. After a few rounds, investors can control the board, which means they can replace the CEO, including a founder.
- Pro rata rights let an investor put money into future rounds to keep their percentage. Early investors care a lot about this, because it lets them put more money into the winners.
- Founders' own shares usually vest over four years, so a founder who leaves after a year doesn't keep everything.

## Part 6: How VCs make money

To understand why VCs behave the way they do, you have to look at their own deal.

A VC fund raises money from **limited partners** (LPs): pension funds, endowments, insurance companies, rich families. The VC firm itself is the **general partner** (GP), which picks the startups. A fund usually lasts about ten years. The classic fee is "2 and 20": 2% of the fund each year for running costs, and 20% of the profits at the end.

The key fact about VC returns is how uneven they are. Horsley Bridge, which invests in many VC funds, looked at about 7,000 venture investments made between 1985 and 2014[^powerlaw]:

About half of the investments lost money. Around 6% returned ten times or more, and those 6% made about 60% of all the returns[^powerlaw]. This is called a **power law**: a few outliers matter more than everything else combined.

This explains a lot of VC behavior that looks strange from outside.

- A VC doesn't want a company that will safely grow into a \$50 million business. That doesn't move the fund. They want a small chance at a \$10 billion company.
- So they push startups to grow fast, raise more and take more risk, even if a slower path would be safer for the founders.
- They accept that most of their companies will fail. Losing money on half the deals is normal. Missing the one big winner is the real mistake.

ARD and DEC in 1957 was the first clear example. One \$70,000 check made the fund.

## Part 7: Raising money in Vietnam

### The numbers

Vietnam's startup scene is much younger and much smaller. Over the ten years from 2014 to 2023, Vietnamese startups raised about \$4.6 billion in total across 835 deals[^vn2021], which is less than some single US companies raise in one round. Year by year:

2021 was the peak, with about \$1.4 billion across 165 deals[^vn2021]. That was the year of cheap money everywhere, and two Vietnamese companies became unicorns. MoMo, the e-wallet, raised a \$200 million Series E led by Mizuho at a valuation above \$2 billion[^momo]. Sky Mavis, the maker of Axie Infinity, raised \$152 million in a Series B led by Andreessen Horowitz, at a reported valuation near \$3 billion[^skymavis].

Then it fell. \$634 million in 2022, \$529 million in 2023, \$398 million in 2024[^vn2022][^vn2023][^vn2024]. Most of the money is also concentrated in a few big rounds: in 2023, deals of \$10 million or more were 69% of the total[^vn2023].

### Why so many "Vietnamese" startups are Singaporean

If you look up who actually raised the money, a lot of Vietnamese startups are legally Singapore companies with a Vietnamese subsidiary. The reason is that the tools in Parts 4 and 5 are hard to use under Vietnamese law. Foreign VC funds want preferred shares, liquidation preferences, anti-dilution, employee stock options and drag-along clauses, and many of these are unfamiliar or awkward in Vietnam[^tuoitre]. Receiving foreign capital is slow, intellectual property is hard to value or use as collateral, and closing a company is a long process. In Singapore all of this is routine, so investors ask founders to set up there before they put money in.

Sky Mavis co-founder Nguyễn Thành Trung put the problem simply: "The biggest difficulties are mechanisms for receiving foreign capital and valuing intellectual property"[^tuoitre]. There are proposals to fix this, including recognizing international investment instruments and an exchange for innovative companies planned for 2027, but for now most founders still go to Singapore.

## Conclusion

Startup fundraising is a trade. Founders give up part of the company, and some control, in exchange for money they couldn't get any other way. Investors take on huge risk in exchange for a small chance at a huge return. The words in the headlines (seed, Series A, valuation) are just the visible part of that deal. The terms you don't see in the headline, like liquidation preferences, decide who actually gets paid.

It also explains why the startup world looks the way it does. VCs need outliers, so they fund companies that try to become very big, very fast. That's how you get Google and DEC, and it's also how you get a company sold for \$425 million where the employees walk away with almost nothing.
