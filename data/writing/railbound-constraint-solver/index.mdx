I came across [Railbound](https://afterburn.games/railbound/) on YouTube, tried it myself, and really liked it. It is a cozy puzzle game about tiny trains. You get a small grid, a few numbered cars, an exit, and a limited number of track pieces. You lay the track, press play, and watch. If the cars leave in order (1, then 2, then 3) and nothing crashes, you win.

Pretty soon I stopped wanting to solve the levels and started wanting to write something that solves them for me. A few years ago I did the same thing to [Hay Day's production schedule](/writing/hayday-game-schedule), so this is apparently a pattern.

So here is a computer playing level 1-15A:

Nobody told it how to solve this. I only described what the game's rules are. The rest of this post is about how that works, and how the game kept trying to break it.

The code is on [GitHub](https://github.com/Th1nhNg0/railbound_cp), written in [MiniZinc](https://www.minizinc.org/).

## Don't solve it, describe it

There are two ways to write a puzzle solver.

**The hard way:** write the game engine, then a search. Place a piece, simulate the trains, see if it worked, undo, try another piece. Then spend weeks adding tricks so it finishes before the heat death of the universe.

**The lazy way:** write down what a solved puzzle looks like, and hand that to a solver that is very good at searching. That is **constraint programming**. You declare the unknowns (what is on each cell, where each car is at each moment) and the rules they must follow. You never say how to search.

I picked the lazy way. The nice side effect is that the model ends up reading like the game's rulebook. When the game invents a new mechanic, I write down one more rule.

## Every empty cell is a question

The board is a grid of variables. Each empty cell asks one question: *which piece goes here?* There are 15 possible answers:

<figure>
  <img
    src={pieces.src}
    alt="The 15 pieces a cell can hold: empty, two straights, four corners and eight switches"
    width={pieces.width}
    height={pieces.height}
    loading="lazy"
  />
  <figcaption>The 15 options for a free cell: nothing, two straights, four corners, eight switches.</figcaption>
</figure>

In MiniZinc that is one line:

```minizinc
array[1..H, 1..W] of var Piece: grid;
```

The full `Piece` type actually has 37 values, because it also includes tunnels, rocks, and switches with memory. But those only ever come from the level itself, the player can never place them. Writing that down as a rule cut every free cell from 37 options to 15, and the solver got noticeably faster. A lot of this project turned out to be exactly that: telling the solver what is impossible, so it doesn't waste time on it.

A few more rules keep the grid honest: fixed pieces stay fixed, you can't place more than the level allows, and placed track has to connect to something.

## Time is just another dimension

A pretty layout is worthless if the trains crash on it. So the model also tracks every car at every moment. Time is split into steps, and for each car and each step there is a variable for its row and its column.

    width={filmstrip.width}

  <figcaption>Level 1-1, the easiest level in the game, unrolled over time. To the solver, each row is a separate set of variables.</figcaption>
</figure>

The movement rule says: look at the piece under the car, figure out which side the car leaves from, and move one cell that way. Simplified, it looks like this:

```minizinc
exit_dir = exit_of[piece, entry_dir] /\
next_r = r + dr[exit_dir] /\
next_c = c + dc[exit_dir]
```

Here is the part I find beautiful. `piece` is not a fixed value. It is `grid[r, c]`, which is still unknown. So the solver is **building the track and driving the trains on it at the same time**. It never builds a whole layout, runs it, and watches it fail. The moment it figures out that a corner on one cell would send car 2 into a wall, that option disappears, along with everything that depended on it.

The remaining rules are short:

- Cars must leave in order: car 1 before car 2 before car 3.
- No two cars in the same cell at the same time.
- No two cars swapping places between steps. They would drive straight through each other, which the game, sadly, does not allow.

## Then the game gets mean

World 1 is just track. After that, every world adds something new, and each new thing became its own small file of rules.

**Tunnels** (world 2). Drive into one end, pop out of the other. In the model, entering a tunnel moves the car to the other side in the same step.

<figure>
  <LoopVideo webm={tunnelWebm} mp4={tunnelMp4} width={486} height={486} label="Level 2-3: two cars loop around and enter a tunnel on the left, then reappear on the top row and drive to the exit" />
  <figcaption>Level 2-3. Both cars use the green tunnel to jump to the top row.</figcaption>
</figure>

**Gates and buttons** (world 3). A gate blocks the track until a car rolls over its button. Now the solver has to care about **when** things happen, not just **where**. Each gate gets a yes/no variable per time step: open or closed.

<figure>
  <LoopVideo webm={gatesWebm} mp4={gatesMp4} width={576} height={486} label="Level 3-7: cars drive over colored buttons, which open the matching gates so the other car can pass" />
  <figcaption>Level 3-7. Each colored dot is a button, and it opens the gate of the same color.</figcaption>
</figure>

**Switches with memory** (worlds 4 and 6). Some switches flip when you press a button. Others flip every time a car leaves them, so the same switch sends car 1 left and car 2 right.

**Stations** (world 5). A car has to stop at its own station and wait there for two steps before it can leave.

**Decoys** (world 7). Extra cars that follow the same rules but don't need to leave. They wander around and get in your way.

And then the game combines all of it. This is 7-7C: three tunnel pairs, five switches that flip whenever a car leaves them, rocks everywhere, and a yellow decoy with its own plans:

<figure>
  <LoopVideo webm={bossWebm} mp4={bossMp4} width={846} height={846} label="Level 7-7C: three cars and a yellow decoy weave through tunnels and self-flipping switches on a 9 by 9 grid full of rocks" />
  <figcaption>Level 7-7C. I would not have found this by hand. The solver took 42 seconds.</figcaption>
</figure>

Most mechanics follow the same recipe: one yes/no variable per object per time step, a rule for how it changes from one step to the next, and the movement rule reads it. Gates, buttons and both kinds of switches all fit that recipe.

**Semaphores** (world 9) did not. They are stop signs that **you** place next to switches, and they open one step after a car enters that switch. So the solver has to decide where they go **and** when they open, and when they open depends on how the cars move, which depends on the semaphores. It took a string of fixes over two days before the model stopped producing either nonsense or nothing.

I did not work all of this out alone. A lot of it came out of a thread on the [Railbound Discord server](https://discord.com/channels/441217491612598272/1142318326136180796), where I worked through the mechanics together with another player from the community.

## So how fast is it?

I ran every puzzle in worlds 1 to 8 through the CP-SAT solver from Google's OR-Tools. That is 151 puzzles, including the hard A, B and C variants. All 151 solved.

The slowest puzzles are not the biggest ones. They are the ones where **timing** matters most. Stations make cars stop, and every stop means more possible timelines to check.

The champion is 5-4C, from the stations world. It took 80 seconds and about 286 million propagation steps, which is the solver's way of saying "hmm" 286 million times:

<figure>
  <LoopVideo webm={nemesisWebm} mp4={nemesisMp4} width={576} height={666} label="Level 5-4C: three cars visit their yellow-numbered stations, wait, then thread through a long shared layout to the exit" />
  <figcaption>Level 5-4C, the hardest puzzle for the solver. Cars 1 and 3 must stop at their numbered stations before leaving.</figcaption>
</figure>

That lines up with how the game feels to play. The levels I found hardest were also the solver's hardest.

In July I spent a day trying to make it faster. Most of the speedup came from **deleting** things: lookup tables that were never used, checks the solver did not need, and tighter limits on when each car can arrive. The full set went from 764 seconds to 632, about 17% faster.

## What I learned

**Write the rules, not the search.** The model is about 1,300 lines of MiniZinc and almost none of it is about how to search. Whenever I got stuck, the fix was to describe the game more precisely.

**Saying what's impossible is the best optimization.** A free cell can't hold a tunnel. A car can't arrive before step `t`. A semaphore can't sit on a gate. Each of those is one line, and each one removes a huge chunk of work.

**Time is the expensive part.** Placing track is easy. Tracking every car, gate and switch at every step is where the variables pile up.

**Mostly, it was fun.** Watching the cars roll through a layout the solver came up with never got old. This was one of the most fun things I have built.

Worlds 10 to 12 add more mechanics, including levels with two separate destinations, and they are still on my list. If you want to try the solver on your own level, the [data format](https://github.com/Th1nhNg0/railbound_cp/blob/main/docs/data_format.md) is documented in the repo. Also check out [FoxtrotOnce's RailboundSolver](https://github.com/FoxtrotOnce/RailboundSolver), a different, logic-driven approach, and where much of the game art in my repo comes from.
