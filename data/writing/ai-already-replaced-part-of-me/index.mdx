Yeah. About that.

I haven't written a single line of code by hand in two, maybe three years. I still build things all the time. I just don't type the code anymore. I read it, and I tell an AI what to write next.

This is not a hot take or a guide. It's a diary entry. I wanted to write down what this actually feels like before I forget what it felt like before.

## How I got here

I grew up on competitive programming. A few years later I was writing posts about C++ tricks for contests. Then AI showed up, and at first it was a toy that wrote a blog post for me. Now it's in everything I do.

It didn't happen in one day. AI took over one small task at a time, and somewhere along the way the person writing the code stopped being me.

## What I handed over

Coding. Searching. Research. Reading documentation. Even brainstorming. All of it goes through AI now, either fully or as an assistant sitting next to me.

And it's not only code anymore. When I hit a topic I don't understand, whether it's a law, an economics paper or some corner of math, I ask AI to explain it, then ask again, then ask it to quiz me. I learn faster than I ever did from textbooks. AI stopped being a coding tool for me a while ago. It's how I learn anything.

My job shrank to two things: reading what the AI produced, and deciding what happens next.

And honestly? I'm happy about it.

## The good part

AI is great at the boring parts. Boilerplate, glue code, the fifth config file, chasing a typo through a stack trace. I don't miss any of that.

What I got back is time to try ideas. Things I would never have started because they'd take a month of evenings now take a weekend. I get to be curious again, just at a different level.

## The part I miss

Here is the part that stings a little.

I don't enjoy writing code and solving problems the way I used to. I can't really code "normally" anymore, and that's not even what bothers me. What bothers me is that the joy left before the skill did.

Competitive programming used to be the most fun thing in my life. Reading a problem, staring at the ceiling, and then the moment it clicks. The last time I really felt that was seven or eight years ago.

Now it feels pointless. Why grind a problem for two hours when the person next to you pastes it into a chatbot and gets an accepted solution in ten seconds? The game didn't change. The meaning did.

The one exception is my [Railbound solver](/writing/railbound-constraint-solver). It was the first time in years that a problem felt fun again, even though AI wrote the code. Maybe the fun was never in the typing.

## It's not just me

This is bigger than one guy who stopped typing code.

- In 2026, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Microsoft plan to spend [about $650 billion](https://www.datacenterknowledge.com/hyperscalers/big-tech-to-spend-650b-this-year-as-ai-race-intensifies) on capital expenditure, much of it AI data centers. That's roughly 60% more than the year before.
- A [Stanford study](https://leaddev.com/hiring/young-devs-steepest-losses-exposed-roles-stanford-study-finds) of US payroll data found employment for 22 to 25 year olds in the most AI-exposed jobs, software developers included, fell 13%. The people getting replaced first are the ones who were where I was a few years ago.
- Vietnam passed its [first AI law](https://en.vneconomy.vn/national-assembly-approves-vietnams-first-ai-law.htm) in December 2025, in force since March 2026. One line stuck with me: AI "must be subject to human oversight in critical decision-making processes."

Which brings me to the one thing I'm not giving away.

## The part I won't hand over

AI can't decide for you, and it shouldn't.

I see this around me more and more. People ask AI what to think, what to buy, what to do, and then just do it. No opinion of their own. The AI isn't assisting their decision. It *is* the decision.

That's the line for me. AI can write my code, read my docs and pitch me ideas. But the last step, choosing, stays mine. If I give that away too, there isn't much "me" left to replace.

So yes, AI already replaced part of me. The typing part, the searching part, maybe even the contest kid. I'm keeping the part that decides.
